Water, food and power in North Africa — and what Morocco does next

Morocco has officially crossed the water-scarcity threshold. The Sahel is drying. Egypt is locked in a dam war with Ethiopia. Lake Chad has shrunk 90%. This is not a distant crisis — it is the frame inside which every economic and security decision in the region is made.

Five things to know

  • Morocco is officially water-scarce. With 730 m³ per person per year, it is below the UN scarcity threshold of 1 000 m³ — and population growth is tightening the squeeze.
  • Farming takes 85% of Morocco's water but produces only 12% of GDP. The political economy of reform is brutal: irrigated agriculture supports 5 million rural livelihoods.
  • Morocco has 149 large dams — one of the highest dam densities in Africa. Yet the 2023 drought still exposed catastrophic fragility.
  • Morocco's answer is desalination: targeting 1 billion m³/year by 2030, powered partly by solar. If it works, it fundamentally changes the country's water arithmetic.
  • Regional instability is a water story. Lake Chad's collapse enabled Boko Haram. Nile dam politics could trigger conflict between Egypt and Ethiopia. What happens in the Sahel does not stay there.

The four drivers

Why water is the new oil

Four forces are converging simultaneously, and Morocco sits at the intersection of all of them.

  1. The climate signal is unambiguous

    Average rainfall across Morocco has fallen 30% since 1980. All major IPCC scenarios project a further 10–30% decline by 2050 for the MENA region. The Atlas snowpack — which recharges the major rivers — is retreating. This is not a temporary cycle; it is a structural shift that planning must accept as the baseline.

  2. Agriculture can't carry this burden indefinitely

    Irrigated agriculture uses 85% of Morocco's water to generate 12% of GDP. The Souss-Massa aquifer, which feeds citrus and tomato exports to Europe, is being overdrawn at three times its recharge rate. Plan Maroc Vert has converted 450 000 ha to drip irrigation — but efficiency gains can only go so far without also addressing what is grown and where.

  3. Shared rivers mean shared conflict

    The Nile, Niger and Lake Chad basins are multinational systems. Ethiopia's Grand Renaissance Dam (GERD) will cut peak Nile flow to Egypt by up to 25% during filling — a near-existential threat for a country 97% dependent on the Nile. Lake Chad has lost 90% of its surface since 1960, displacing millions and providing the grievance environment in which Boko Haram flourished. What happens upstream does not stay upstream.

  4. Food imports are a geopolitical exposure

    Morocco imports roughly 38% of its wheat. It learned how exposed that is when Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine spiked global grain prices 60% overnight. Food price shocks historically correlate with social unrest in the MENA region (2007–08, 2010–11 Arab Spring). Water efficiency and domestic production are, ultimately, security investments.

Transboundary risk

Shared waters, rising tensions

Nile Basin

Ethiopia, Egypt and the GERD

The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile will be the largest dam in Africa when complete. Ethiopia sees it as the engine of industrialisation. Egypt, whose 106 million people depend on the Nile for 97% of their water, sees it as an existential threat. Sudan sits in between, sometimes aligned with each side. Three rounds of AU-mediated talks have failed to produce a binding legal framework. The risk of military escalation is real — Egypt has rehearsed strike scenarios.

Lake Chad Basin

The lake that nearly disappeared

Lake Chad has lost 90% of its surface area since 1960 — driven by climate change and upstream irrigation abstraction by Nigeria, Niger, Chad and Cameroon. Thirty million people who depended on the lake for fishing, farming and livestock have faced mass displacement. Scholars link the lake's collapse directly to the social conditions that enabled Boko Haram's insurgency from 2009. The Lake Chad Basin Commission has a replenishment plan, but implementation requires financing and political will from four fragile states.

Niger River

Nine countries, one artery

The Niger River flows 4 200 km through Guinea, Mali, Niger and Nigeria. It supports 110 million people. Guinea's upstream dams threaten downstream flow; Mali's Office du Niger irrigation scheme already abstracts heavily. After the 2023 Sahel coups, regional governance institutions — including the Niger River Authority — have been weakened. If upstream states pursue unilateral dam construction, downstream droughts will intensify.

NWSA Aquifer

The Sahara's fossil water

The North-Western Sahara Aquifer System (NWSA) is shared by Algeria, Tunisia and Libya. It holds an estimated 60 000 km³ of water — but almost none is replenished. Libya's Great Man-Made River pumps it at scale; Algeria and Tunisia abstract heavily for irrigation. Once exhausted, this water is gone. No binding international agreement governs its extraction. It is the slowest-moving water crisis in the region, and among the least discussed.

Policy response

Morocco's $40 billion water bet

Morocco's National Water Programme 2020–2050 commits MAD 380 billion (~$40 billion) over three decades. Four pillars.

Desalination

From 0.3 to 1 Gm³/year by 2030

Morocco is building a network of large seawater desalination plants along the Atlantic and Mediterranean coasts. The Casablanca plant (300 Mm³/yr) began operation in 2026. Agadir (450 Mm³/yr) and Dakhla plants follow. Solar-powered desalination reduces the energy cost. Critically, desalination water is priced higher — which creates an incentive to reserve cheaper fresh water for lower-cost uses. Challenge: coastal infrastructure only serves coastal cities; pumping inland is expensive.

Drip irrigation

Plan Maroc Vert — 700 000 ha target

Morocco launched drip irrigation conversion in 2008. By 2024, ~550 000 ha had been converted — roughly a third of irrigated land. Pilot results show 40–60% water savings per hectare. However, critics note that saved water often gets absorbed by expanded area under irrigation, not freed for ecosystems or cities. The Génération Green 2020–2030 plan targets 700 000 ha. The monitoring and governance of actual aggregate water use remains the weak link.

Wastewater reuse

Treated water for agriculture

Morocco generates about 750 Mm³ of wastewater annually and currently reuses less than 5%. The national water plan targets 325 Mm³/yr reused by 2030, primarily for irrigating golf courses, green spaces and some crops. Israel reuses over 87% of wastewater — making it a model. The barrier in Morocco is infrastructure cost and public acceptance for food crops.

Interbasin transfer

Moving water from north to south

Northern Morocco (Sebou, Loukkos basins) receives more rainfall than the south. A network of inter-basin transfer tunnels and canals already moves water from the Sebou basin to the Bouregreg (Rabat water supply). Plans exist to extend transfers to the Oum Er-Rbia and Tensift basins, which supply Marrakech and the Haouz plain. The challenge: northern surpluses are also declining as northern rainfall drops.

Looking ahead

Three futures for Morocco

Base case

Managed scarcity

Desalination scales to 1 Gm³/yr by 2030. Drip irrigation reaches 700 000 ha. Rainfall continues declining at current rate. Morocco remains water-scarce but avoids catastrophic shortage — at high financial cost and with persistent agricultural vulnerability.

Desalination leap

Gulf-style investment (Saudi sovereign fund, UAE desal expertise) scales Moroccan desalination to 2.5 Gm³/yr by 2035. Rainfall stabilises. Water stress eases enough to allow genuine agricultural modernisation and attract water-intensive green hydrogen industry.

Indicators to follow

What to watch

  • Al Massira fill ratePublished weekly by Morocco's Ministry of Water. When it falls below 15%, expect emergency rationing orders for Casablanca. Track at eau.gov.ma.
  • National reservoir averageMorocco's 149 dams combined. Below 30% signals national drought emergency. 2023 hit 23%; 2026 recovery to ~38% after better Atlantic rains.
  • Casablanca desal ramp-upThe 300 Mm³/yr plant online in 2026. Watch monthly production vs. design capacity — early desal plants globally operate at 60–70% of nameplate for 2 years.
  • GERD filling scheduleEach km³ Ethiopia fills reduces Nile flow downstream. Egypt has signalled a red line at sustained <40 billion m³/yr reaching Aswan. Watch AU mediation rounds.
  • Souss-Massa water tableMonitored by the Agence du bassin hydraulique. The piezometric level in key wells signals how close the aquifer is to economic depletion of the irrigated export belt.
  • Sahel rainfall (ITCZ shift)The Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone has been shifting north erratically. A stable northward shift would bring more rainfall to the Sahel but alter Atlantic weather patterns for Morocco. Watch NOAA and ECMWF seasonal forecasts.
