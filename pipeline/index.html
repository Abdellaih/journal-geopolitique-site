How a $25 billion pipeline could make Morocco Africa's energy bridge

A 6,800-kilometre pipeline along the Atlantic coast. Thirteen countries. Thirty billion cubic metres of gas per year. If it gets built, the African Atlantic Gas Pipeline will be the longest subsea pipeline in the world — and it ends in Morocco, giving Europe a new route away from Russian gas and giving Africa's most gas-rich nation a way to stop burning its wealth.

The short version

  • Nigeria flares gas worth billions of dollars a year for lack of a way to sell it. The pipeline fixes that.
  • West Africa has 400 million people with unreliable electricity. Tapping into the pipeline while it passes is a condition of every country's support.
  • Europe needs alternatives to Russian gas after 2022. Morocco is already connected to Spain by two existing pipelines — the NMGP plugs into them.
  • Morocco sits at the junction. It collects transit fees, deepens ties with 13 neighbours, and becomes the continent's gas hub.
  • The obstacles are real: $25 billion needs investors, several route sections cross politically fragile territory, and the Western Sahara segment is contested.

The three problems it solves

Why this pipeline, why now

Three separate energy crises, all pointing to the same answer.

  1. Nigeria's wasted gas

    Nigeria has the largest proven gas reserves in Africa — about 209 trillion cubic feet. It also flares more gas than almost any country on earth: over 7 billion cubic metres burned at the wellhead in 2023, equivalent to roughly a quarter of Morocco's entire annual energy consumption. The cause is simple: no pipes to move it.

  2. West Africa's power gap

    Over 600 million people in sub-Saharan Africa lack reliable electricity. Every country the pipeline passes through — Benin, Togo, Ghana, Ivory Coast, Liberia, Sierra Leone, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Gambia, Senegal, Mauritania — has agreed to offtake points because gas-fired generation is the fastest way to close their power deficit.

  3. Europe's Russian gas problem

    After the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, the EU cut Russian pipeline gas from 40% of its supply to near zero. LNG from the US and Qatar filled the gap — at a premium. A new pipeline from a stable African supplier is exactly what European energy ministers have been asking for. Morocco connects to Spain via the Maghreb-Europe Gas pipeline (MEG) and the Pedro Duran Farell pipeline, with combined capacity of about 14 bcm/yr.

  4. Morocco's strategic window

    The pipeline enters Europe through Morocco, not Algeria. That is not an accident: Algiers closed the MEG pipeline to Morocco in 2021 citing the breakdown in diplomatic relations. Rabat now positions itself as the reliable Atlantic transit country. Being the junction gives Morocco leverage, transit revenue, and a claim to regional energy leadership it did not have before.

What could go wrong

Five obstacles

Financing

$25 billion needs investors

No final investment decision has been taken. Western development banks are cautious about new gas infrastructure given net-zero commitments. The project company will be based in Casablanca; the governing authority in Abuja. Attracting private capital depends on bankable offtake contracts from 13 states.

Political fragility

Coups and instability en route

The pipeline passes through Guinea and Guinea-Bissau, both of which have experienced coups in recent years. Liberia and Sierra Leone face governance challenges. A single failed state on the route can disrupt supply or make insurance unaffordable.

Western Sahara

Disputed territory on the route

Between Mauritania and Morocco proper lies Western Sahara, whose sovereignty is contested. Morocco administers it; the Polisario Front claims it. Infrastructure built there is legally and diplomatically sensitive, affecting European offtake contracts.

Competition

LNG and other pipelines

Europe is locking in long-term LNG contracts from the US and Qatar. The Trans-Saharan pipeline (Algeria–Nigeria) is a competing route that Algeria has backed for decades. If LNG prices fall or Algeria restores ties with Europe, the case for the NMGP weakens.

Timeline

First gas in 2031 — optimistic

Construction of 6,800 km of mostly subsea pipeline across 13 jurisdictions has never been done before. The IGA was signed in July 2026; a final investment decision, procurement, and build typically take 6–10 years. First gas before 2033–2035 would be exceptional.

Delayed but built

The Moroccan angle

Why this matters most for Morocco

Morocco imports all of its gas and nearly all its oil. It has no refinery and no pipeline from a producing country — a structural vulnerability that the Hormuz shock of 2026 exposed. The NMGP changes that equation in three ways.

Energy security

A domestic gas supply

Morocco currently imports LNG from Spain, which re-exports US and Qatari cargoes. Owning a pipeline offtake gives Rabat a direct, long-term supply that cannot be interrupted by spot-market volatility or the closure of a distant strait.

Transit hub

Fees and geopolitical leverage

Every cubic metre bound for Europe passes through Moroccan territory and the MEG connection to Spain. Transit fees are estimated at $0.05–0.08/MMBtu per 1,000 km — on 15 bcm/yr to Europe, that is hundreds of millions of dirhams annually. More importantly, being the gate gives Morocco a seat at every European energy table.

Regional leadership

Africa's energy bridge

The project company headquarters in Casablanca and the diplomatic architecture Morocco built with 13 ECOWAS states lock in its role as the continent's Atlantic energy anchor. This reinforces Morocco's broader Africa strategy — re-joining the African Union in 2017, the Atlantic Africa summit in 2023, the Rabat process on Sahel stability.

Indicators to follow

What to watch

  • Final Investment DecisionTarget is 2027. No FID = no pipeline. Watch for first bankable offtake contract from an EU buyer.
  • Project company, CasablancaThe company was mandated at the ECOWAS summit. Its formation and first board meeting are the next concrete milestones.
  • Trans-Saharan pipelineAlgeria's competing route. Any rapprochement between Algiers and Rabat — or Algiers and the EU — changes the competitive map.
  • European gas pricesAbove $10/MMBtu the NMGP is competitive. Below $8, LNG from the US becomes cheaper and harder to displace.
  • Guinea & Guinea-Bissau stabilityTwo coups in the last five years on the route. Political crises trigger force-majeure clauses and deter investors.
  • Western Sahara vote, Oct 31The MINURSO mandate renewal at the UN (due October 31, 2026) affects the legal and diplomatic status of the pipeline's most contested segment.
