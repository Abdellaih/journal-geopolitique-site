From Hormuz
to the pump

How a war in the Gulf reaches a Moroccan fuel tank in about two weeks

Oil is the most political commodity in the world. A strait closes, a pipeline burns, a cartel loses a member, and the price in London moves within minutes. Moroccan pumps follow at the next fortnightly revision. This page traces that chain, using 2026, the year the Strait of Hormuz shut, as the worked example.

Legend · the short version

  • In the short run nobody can quickly use less oil or pump more, so losing a few percent of supply forces a large price jump.
  • Geopolitics works through six channels: outages, chokepoints, sanctions, cartel decisions, fear, and the cost of shipping and dollars.
  • Morocco imports finished fuel. It feels diesel and gasoline prices, freight and the dollar, with a lag of about two weeks.
  • Shock absorbers decide whether a spike lasts days or months: spare capacity, bypass pipelines, emergency stocks and falling demand.
  • In Morocco, inflation looks low because food got cheaper and the state absorbed part of the shock. The bill shows up instead in the budget, the trade deficit and slower non-farm growth.

The core mechanism

Why a small shortage makes a big spike

The world burns about 104 million barrels of oil a day. In the short run, almost nobody can change that. Trucks still have to deliver, harvests need diesel, and a new oil field takes years to develop. Economists say demand and supply are inelastic.

So when a few percent of supply goes missing, the price has to climb a long way before enough buyers give up their barrels. A rough rule of thumb: lose 1% of world supply for a sustained period and, if nothing else changes, prices rise by around 10%.

In March 2026 the market lost about 10 million barrels a day, the largest disruption on record. The rule of thumb says Brent should have roughly doubled. It did spike, to $119 in early March and $126 at the end of April, but it averaged about $94 between the start of the war and mid-August. The gap is the work of shock absorbers, covered further down.

The channels

Six levers geopolitics pulls

Politics changes the oil price by changing how many barrels the market expects to get, and how much it costs to deliver them. It does that in six ways.

Physical outages

Missiles, drones and sabotage take wells, refineries and pipelines offline. Every lost barrel has to be replaced from somewhere else.

2026Saudi Arabia shut its largest domestic refinery after a drone strike in the first days of the war. Attacks closed its East-West export pipeline in September.Before2019: the Abqaiq attack briefly removed 5.7 mb/d.

Chokepoints

Most oil travels by sea through a few narrow straits. Close one and the oil still exists, but it cannot reach buyers.

2026Hormuz, normally about a fifth of world oil, has been largely shut since early March.Before2023–24: Red Sea attacks sent tankers around Africa.

Sanctions & blockades

Governments decide who may sell to whom. Barrels rarely vanish. They take longer routes on older tankers at a discount, and the detour costs money.

2026A US naval blockade of Iranian ports runs alongside the Hormuz closure.Before2022–23: EU bans on Russian crude and diesel reshuffled world trade.

Cartel decisions

OPEC+ members agree production quotas. Adding or holding back barrels is a political choice as much as an economic one.

2026The UAE left OPEC and OPEC+ on 1 May. The seven core members still coordinating held November output flat at 31.01 mb/d.Before1973 embargo; market-share wars in 2014 and 2020.

The fear premium

Futures traders price the chance of losing barrels before any are lost. Headlines move prices both ways, sometimes 5–7% in a day.

2026Brent rose more than 13% right after the 28 February strikes, then swung with every round of US–Iran talks.Before2019: the Abqaiq premium faded within two weeks.

Freight, insurance & the dollar

War-risk insurance and longer voyages add dollars to every barrel delivered. Oil is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar raises the bill for countries paying in dirhams.

The Moroccan chain

From the barrel to your litre

Morocco has not refined crude since the SAMIR refinery at Mohammedia stopped in 2015, and pump prices have been free since the end of that year. Distributors import finished gasoil and essence, so six steps decide what you pay.

  1. Crude is priced in London

    Brent futures react to news within seconds, around the clock.

  2. Refiners add their premium

    The crack spread is what refiners earn turning crude into diesel or gasoline. It jumps when refineries are hit or fuel exports stop.

  3. Ships and insurers add theirs

    War-risk cover and longer routes add dollars to every barrel landed in Morocco.

  4. Dollars become dirhams

    The dirham follows a basket of 60% euro and 40% dollar, within a ±5% band.

  5. The state adds tax

    TIC is a fixed 2.42 DH per litre of gasoil and 3.76 DH of essence. VAT of 10% comes on top.

  6. Distributors set the pump price

    Margins and logistics are added, and prices are revised on the 1st and 16th of each month.

Each step below is a fortnightly revision. The pattern follows the war: a slide while a deal looked close in June, a floor in early July, then three months of increases as attacks resumed.

The shock absorbers

Why oil did not hit $200

The largest supply loss in history produced a smaller spike than many analysts expected. Five things soaked up part of it.

Bypass pipelines

Saudi Arabia's East-West line to the Red Sea and the UAE's line to Fujairah carry some Gulf oil around Hormuz. Producers rerouted what they could, until the East-West line was itself attacked in September.

Emergency stocks

In March, G7 finance ministers said they were ready to release strategic reserves. The promise alone calms traders.

Demand destruction

High prices make people and firms use less. The World Bank estimates world consumption in March was 0.8 mb/d lower than a year earlier.

Supply from elsewhere

High prices pull in oil from outside the Gulf. The EIA expects US crude output to average 13.6 mb/d in 2026. Outside OPEC quotas, the UAE could add up to 1.6 mb/d once Hormuz reopens.

Diplomacy, priced in

Every sign of a US–Iran deal pulls prices down and caps rallies. Brent fell 7% in a single session on peace-talk hopes.

Result: Brent averaged about $94 from the start of the war to mid-August, well below the $150 some feared.

Rockets and feathers?

Drivers everywhere suspect pump prices rise fast and fall slowly. In Morocco the evidence points two ways, because the two sides measure different things.

Fortnight by fortnight

The Conseil de la concurrence found pump prices tracked international quotes closely from 1 March to 16 May 2026. For essence, world prices rose 2.81 DH/L and the pump rose 2.43. In the first half of March, gasoil quotes rose 2.92 DH/L and pumps 2.03.

The level of the margin

Critics look at the margin itself. One analysis published by Ecoactu estimates the average gross margin on gasoil rose from about 1.05 DH/L in 2022 to about 2.02, even when changes were passed on.

The ripple effects

From the pump to the whole economy

Fuel is an input to almost everything that moves. A price shock that starts at the pump spreads through the economy in four stages, each slower and harder to reverse than the last.

  1. The direct hit

    Households and firms pay more for fuel, cooking gas and electricity, unless the state absorbs the increase.

  2. Costs spread

    Diesel moves goods, so trucking, food distribution, farming, fishing, construction and fertilizer all get dearer within weeks.

  3. Second-round effects

    Firms raise prices to protect margins, workers ask for higher pay, and central banks may raise rates to stop inflation settling in.

  4. The macro bill

    Growth slows, the trade deficit widens as more money pays for imported energy, and subsidies strain the budget.

The IMF's July update assumed oil around $89 and energy prices 25% above pre-war levels. Growth in the Middle East and Central Asia was cut to 0.7% for 2026, and euro-area growth to 0.9%. Global trade growth slows from 5% in 2025 to 3.5%. Commodity importers that are not riding the AI investment boom were generally downgraded.

The shock also reaches the dinner table. Fertilizer made from Gulf gas, ammonia and sulfur got sharply dearer, and the World Bank reports farmers' ability to afford fertilizer at its weakest since mid-2022. Higher fertilizer costs today mean higher food costs at the next harvests.

Fuel prices jumped 9.8% in August alone, and transport costs are up 7.3% on the year. Headline inflation still fell, because a cereal harvest of about 93 million quintals pushed food prices down 3.9%. Without that harvest, the fuel shock would be far more visible in the index.

Growth is holding up for the same reason: farm output is expected to jump 16%. The non-farm economy, which feels fuel costs most, slows to about 3.1% from 4.5% in 2025.

Who absorbed the shock

Transport aid has been renewed in later rounds as pump prices kept rising. These costs explain why Bank Al-Maghrib now sees the 2027 budget deficit at 3.5% of GDP instead of 3.3%.

One mixed exposure: Morocco's phosphate fertilizers sell for more when fertilizer prices rise, but OCP also imports sulfur and ammonia, and sulfur prices doubled this year.

Bank Al-Maghrib kept its policy rate at 2.25% on 22 September. With inflation near 1%, it has room to wait. Its own assumption for Brent is $87.2 on average in 2026 and $73.5 in 2027, so a war that drags on would push its numbers the wrong way.

Concerns that outlast the war

Six things to keep an eye on

Food, 2027

Food and energy together

This year a big harvest hid the fuel shock. Bank Al-Maghrib assumes the cereal crop falls to about 50 million quintals in 2027 and core inflation rises to 2.2%. Dearer fertilizer adds to that risk.

Interest rates

Inflation that sticks

The IMF warns oil-driven inflation could shift expectations and tighten financial conditions. The ECB has already raised rates, and Bank Al-Maghrib lists rising government borrowing costs among its risks.

Public finances

The cost of the shield

Subsidies cost over 1.6 bn DH a month at their spring peak. A long war means choosing between higher deficits, cuts elsewhere, or letting prices rise.

External balance

More dirhams leaving

Morocco imports most of its energy. A 28% bigger energy bill helps push the current account deficit from 2.4% to 4.6% of GDP, which draws on foreign currency.

Energy security

Thin stocks

In May the energy minister put gasoil stocks at 48 days, below the 60-day legal obligation; operators are reluctant to fill tanks at high prices. The war revived the debate over SAMIR's idle storage and refinery, whose usable capacity is disputed.

Market structure

A permanent risk premium

Iran wants a say over Hormuz traffic and tolls, and the UAE has left OPEC. A weaker cartel and a contested strait mean bigger swings both ways: spikes during crises, gluts after them.
